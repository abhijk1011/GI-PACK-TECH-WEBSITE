## What a vessel liner does

Open-top process equipment — mixing tanks, kettles, blending vessels, storage bins, open reactors, holding tanks — spends a large share of its life being cleaned. Every product change means scraping residue, washing, sometimes solvent cleaning and, in regulated plants, validating that the vessel is clean before the next batch goes in.

A **vessel liner** removes most of that work. It is a flexible film liner made to the vessel's internal diameter and height. The batch sits in the liner, not against the vessel wall. When the batch is done, the liner comes out with the residue in it, and the vessel needs a fraction of the cleaning it would otherwise.

The benefits follow directly:

- **Shorter changeovers** — a liner change instead of a wash and dry.
- **Less cross-contamination** — the next product never meets the last one's residue on the wall.
- **Less cleaning solvent and effluent** — the residue leaves in the liner.
- **Less product lost** — residue that would have been washed away can often be recovered from the liner.
- **Longer vessel life** — the wall is not attacked or scoured on every batch.

## Why it has to be made to the vessel

A liner that is too small strains at the seams under the weight of the batch. One that is too large folds into creases where product is trapped — and in a mixing vessel, loose film near an agitator is a hazard of its own. A vessel liner therefore has to be made to the **measured** vessel: internal diameter, internal height, and the position of anything inside it — agitator shafts, baffles, nozzles, sample points.

## The limits of a flexible liner

A flexible liner is for **open-top, non-pressurised** vessels, operating within the temperature range of its film. It does not replace a reactor's permanent lining — glass, PTFE or alloy — for pressure, vacuum or reaction service, and it should not be used where an agitator or heating element would contact or damage it. The film sets the temperature limit; hot charging is a different question from storage.

Send the operating conditions with the enquiry. Where a flexible liner is the right tool, it is specified against them. Where it is not, GI PackTech says so — the company's standing position is that it would rather refuse a job than supply a film that will fail.

## Charging powders into a vessel: the static question

Tipping or discharging a powder into a vessel that contains, or has recently contained, flammable solvent is one of the classic static ignition scenarios in chemical and pharmaceutical plants. The packaging the powder arrives in is part of that hazard:

- A plain plastic bag or liner can carry enough charge to ignite solvent vapour at the vessel opening.
- **Type C** FIBCs must be earthed throughout the discharge; **Type D** FIBCs dissipate charge without an earth connection — see [FIBC types A, B, C and D](/blog/fibc-types-a-b-c-d-explained).
- The liner inside the bag must be compatible with the bag's static protection — a [conductive liner](/products/bulk-bag-and-fibc-liners/conductive-liner) rather than plain polyethylene.

Packaging is one control among several — inerting, earthing, charging systems and procedures — and the process safety assessment decides the whole set. The [static safety guide](/blog/conductive-liners-and-static-safety) covers the packaging side.

- Whether solvent or flammable vapour is present
- How the vessel is charged and emptied
- Batch frequency and annual quantity
