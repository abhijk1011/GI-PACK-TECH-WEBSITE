## What caking actually is

Caking is not a property of the product. It is what happens when moisture reaches the product and the temperature moves.

1. **Moisture uptake.** Hygroscopic materials — fertiliser salts, many chemicals, sugars, some food ingredients — draw water from the air once the relative humidity rises above their **critical relative humidity**. Below it, they stay dry; above it, they absorb.
2. **Surface dissolution.** The absorbed water dissolves a thin film at each particle's surface.
3. **Bridging.** When the temperature drops or the air dries, the dissolved material recrystallises and forms solid bridges between neighbouring particles.
4. **Cycling.** Day and night temperature swings repeat the process, moving moisture around inside the bag and strengthening the bridges every cycle.

For many fertiliser salts, the critical humidity is below the humidity of a monsoon, a tropical port or a ship's hold. So a product that leaves the plant free-flowing can arrive as a solid block — and be sold at a discount, reprocessed or rejected.

## Why polyethylene is not enough

Polyethylene is a reasonable moisture barrier compared with paper or woven fabric, but it is not a stop. Water vapour passes through it slowly, and over weeks the inside of the liner comes to match the humidity outside. Doubling the thickness roughly halves the rate — which delays caking but does not prevent it on a long storage or voyage.

That is the trap: a heavier PE liner seems to work in a dry month and fails in the monsoon.

## The aluminium multilayer liner

An aluminium laminate puts a layer of foil between polymer layers. Intact foil is effectively impermeable to water vapour, so a sealed aluminium liner holds the product at the humidity it was packed at for as long as the seals and the foil stay intact.

| Liner | Moisture barrier | Typical outcome over a humid storage period |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Woven bag, no liner | None | Caking where humidity exceeds the critical point |
| Polyethylene liner | Slows moisture | Caking delayed, often still occurs |
| Thicker polyethylene liner | Slows moisture more | Caking delayed further |
| Sealed aluminium multilayer liner | Near-total | Product stays at its packed condition |

## The seal is where moisture gets in

With the foil doing its job, the remaining paths are the **heat seals** and any **pinholes or flex cracks** from handling. A barrier liner is only as good as its weakest seal. That is why the seal specification — not the layer count — is the real measure of a barrier liner, and where GI PackTech's development work has gone.

## Practical steps beyond the liner

- **Pack dry and cool.** A barrier holds the condition the product was packed in; it does not dry a wet product. Product packed warm will cycle moisture inside the liner as it cools.
- **Seal promptly** after filling, and seal the spout or neck properly — a loosely tied neck undoes the barrier.
- **Store off the floor**, out of direct sun and rain, and away from heat sources that drive temperature cycling.
- **On sea routes**, protect the load from [container rain](/blog/container-rain-and-cargo-sweat).
- **If the product is also flammable**, a [conductive aluminium liner](/products/bulk-bag-and-fibc-liners/conductive-aluminium-liner) gives the barrier and static control in one.

## What to send

- The product, its data sheet and, if known, its critical relative humidity
- Packing temperature and moisture content
- Storage period, route and climate — including monsoon storage
- Container: FIBC dimensions, drum dimensions or sack size

A side-by-side trial — one batch in the current liner, one in the barrier liner, checked at the same interval — is the clearest way to see the difference.
